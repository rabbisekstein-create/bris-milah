About

My story

A profession handed down, generation to generation — and the reason I take it as seriously as I do.

It began at home.

My father was a Mohel, and so were the generations before him, going back many generations in our family. I did not come to this work by chance. I grew up beside it — hearing the phone ring at strange hours, watching my father prepare, seeing how families looked at him when he arrived, and how they looked at him when he left.

Twenty-three years ago I began to learn the profession properly, taught directly by my father and studying alongside some of the top mohalim of our time. The technical part can be taught in a matter of months. The rest — the judgment, the calm, the way you speak to a mother who has not slept in eight days — takes considerably longer, and I am still learning it.

Why I committed fully

For many years, bris milah was part of my life alongside my other work. About a year and a half ago, something changed that.

My uncle, my father’s brother, is a well-known mohel in Antwerp. Police and authorities came to him over the brisen he performs, and the story was covered around the world. Watching it, I realized something I had taken for granted: a mitzvah Jews have kept for close to four thousand years, through every hardship, still cannot be taken for granted today.

That gave me the courage to dedicate myself to it fully. I went to train further with Rabbi Mike Rovinsky of EasyCircumcision, and since then I have performed hundreds of brisen, for newborns and for adult men.

Every bris carries that feeling with it. Whether it’s a baby on his eighth day or a man who waited sixty years, each one keeps the chain going, and it is a privilege to be part of it.

How I work

I answer my own phone. When you call, you get me — not an office, not a service. We talk properly before the day: the timing, the location, the health of the baby, who you would like to honor, and anything at all that is on your mind. Nothing is too small to ask, and no question is one I have not heard before.

On the day itself I arrive early and unhurried, with everything prepared. I explain what is about to happen to whoever wants to hear it, and I keep the moment calm and dignified. A Bris should feel like a simcha, not like a medical appointment that everyone is holding their breath through.

Afterwards, I do not disappear. You get clear aftercare instructions, and I check in over the following days. If anything concerns you, you call me — at any hour. That is what I am here for.

Beyond my own community

Over the years I have been fortunate to travel and serve families in places where there is no local Mohel, including communities across Latin America. I have also trained specifically for adult Bris Milah, which brought me to men who had waited decades for this and had nowhere to turn. Both have taught me the same lesson: the ceremony matters enormously to people, and it deserves to be done properly wherever they are.
