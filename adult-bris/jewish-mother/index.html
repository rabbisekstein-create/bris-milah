Jewish roots

A Jewish mother: matrilineal descent

According to halacha, the child of a Jewish mother is Jewish, whoever the father is. That stays true if you grew up with no connection, were never circumcised, or only found out recently. What usually needs confirming is your mother’s own status, and a rabbi can help with that.

General information onlyEvery case is decided by a rabbi. This page gives general information only.

What matrilineal descent means

In halacha, Jewish identity passes through the mother. Your mother is Jewish if her own mother was Jewish, or if she converted according to halacha. Her children are Jewish too, whether or not the family practised, and whether or not anyone ever spoke about it.

Being Jewish isn’t something you lose by growing up far from it. Many men discover it in their thirties, fifties or seventies. It was always yours.

How it is usually confirmed

What usually needs confirming is your mother’s status, and sometimes her mother’s. A rabbi works from whatever you have, for example:

  • your parents’ or grandparents’ ketubah (Jewish marriage contract)
  • birth, marriage or immigration records that list religion or nationality
  • a grave in a Jewish cemetery, or a family member’s synagogue membership
  • what relatives remember, and family letters or photographs

Sometimes the picture is clear quickly. Sometimes a beis din needs to look more closely. You don’t need any documents to make the first call.

If your mother converted

Then her conversion is part of the question, and a rabbi or beis din will want to know how and where it was done. This is common and nothing to worry about. It simply means one more conversation.

A story worth remembering

I once met a man in Central America, warm, fluent in English, and a great friend of the Jewish people. In the middle of a conversation, we realised that his mother was Jewish. On my next visit, when I told him about my work, something came back to him: he had had a brit when he was about eight years old, more than fifty years earlier, and had been given a Jewish name.

The sad part is that he no longer remembers the name, and there is no certificate to show what was done.

Keep the records

If you, your father or your sons have a brit certificate, a Hebrew name written down, a ketubah or any family document, keep it safe. Take a photo, and give copies to your children. One piece of paper can answer a question decades later.

And the brit?

For a Jewish man who never had a brit, brit milah remains a mitzvah at any age. If you were circumcised in a hospital, a rabbi may advise hatafas dam bris instead. Either way, nothing is arranged until we’ve spoken.

When you’re ready, you can answer a few short questions about your family and I’ll call you back. If only your father is Jewish, read a Jewish father instead.
