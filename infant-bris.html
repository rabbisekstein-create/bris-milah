Infant Bris Milah

The full ceremony on the eighth day — at home, at shul, or wherever your family gathers. Mazel tov.

What is included

One call arranges everything. From the moment we speak, the Bris is my responsibility to organize, and yours to enjoy.

  • An unhurried consultation with both parents beforehand
  • Working out the correct eighth day with you, exactly
  • All sterile, single-use instruments, brought by me
  • Guidance on the ceremony, the honors and the naming
  • The full ceremony conducted according to halacha
  • Written aftercare instructions in plain language
  • A follow-up check in the days afterwards
  • The seudas bris arranged, if you would like
  • My direct number, available to you at any hour

The eighth day

The Bris takes place on the eighth day of the baby’s life, counted from the day of birth — including Shabbos and Yom Tov, where the birth was natural and the baby is well.

Because the Jewish day begins at sunset, a baby born in the evening may have a different eighth day than parents expect. Tell me the exact date and time of birth and I will work it out with you.

A Bris is performed by day, and traditionally as early in the day as is practical.

The ceremony

What actually happens

The gathering usually runs far longer than the Bris itself. The procedure is brief; the ceremony, blessings and naming together typically take around fifteen minutes. Here is the shape of it, so nothing is a surprise.

  1. The honors

    Families traditionally give honors — the kvatter and kvatterin who bring the baby in, the sandek who holds him, and others. We go over all of this beforehand so everyone knows where to stand and what to do.

  2. The blessings and the Bris

    The blessings are recited, and the Bris itself is performed. It is over quickly. I work calmly and without rushing, and I explain what I am doing to whoever wants to know.

  3. The naming

    Your son is given his Hebrew name, with the blessing that he grow to Torah, to chuppah, and to good deeds. For most families this is the moment they remember.

  4. The seudah

    The festive meal that follows is part of the mitzvah. I am glad to stay, and glad to answer any question a guest or grandparent wants to ask.

The seudah

Seudas bris — סעודת ברית

A Bris can be performed without a meal, and the mitzvah is fulfilled. But our tradition does not treat the seudah as decoration. It is brought in halacha that making a seudah on the day of the Bris is itself a mitzvah, and the meal that follows is a seudas mitzvah in its own right — the moment a child’s entry into the bris of Avraham Avinu is not merely carried out, but rejoiced over.

What the child receives from it

  • A mitzvah done with simcha. The same act, done with joy, is not the same act. His Bris is remembered by everyone there as a simcha, not as a procedure.
  • The brachos of everyone present. At the seudah those gathered bless him that he grow to Torah, to chuppah and to maasim tovim. Those brachos are said over him in the first days of his life.
  • The zechus of the assembled. Jews coming together to rejoice at his entry into the covenant is itself a merit, and it accompanies him.
  • A community that has received him. He is not brought into the bris privately. He is brought in before his people, and they sit down together over it.

Every family should have this, and no family should have to explain why they cannot.

If you would like me to arrange the seudah, I can — it simply has to be ordered in advance. And where the cost is a difficulty, for whatever reason, there is often a sponsor. You need only say the word when we speak.

Three ways to do it

The full seudah

Challah for ten, fish, soup, chicken, and two side dishes. A proper sit-down seudah, and what most families choose.

Buffet style

Bagels, salads and the accompaniments. Lighter, and easier where guests are coming and going through the morning.

The full seudah with meat

Everything in the full seudah, with beef added. The fullest of the three, for families who want to mark the day that way.

All three are a seudas mitzvah — what makes a meal a seudah is the bread, and each of these has it. Beyond that, simcha has long been associated with meat and wine, and that is why some families choose to add the beef. Tell me which suits you and I will take care of the ordering.

This is arranged quietly, and it stays between us. Nobody at the simcha will know how the seudah was arranged, and I will never raise it in front of anyone. Ask me early, in private, and it will be taken care of like any other detail of the day.

Afterwards

Healing and aftercare

Before I leave, I show you exactly how to care for the area and what is normal to see over the following days. You will have it in writing as well — nobody remembers instructions given on the morning of a simcha.

Babies typically settle quickly and feed normally. Healing is usually straightforward and well advanced within a week. I check in with you during those days, and if anything at all concerns you before then, you call me. There is no such thing as bothering me about this.

If the baby is unwell, we wait. Jaundice, prematurity, low birth weight, or any question about the baby’s health means the Bris is postponed until he is ready. This is not a compromise — halacha itself requires it. I will always tell you plainly what I think, and where appropriate we will speak with your pediatrician together.
